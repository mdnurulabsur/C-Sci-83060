What If the Formula Changed?

The Times Higher Education rankings reduce everything a university does to a single score. That score depends on five factors and how much weight each one gets. Change the weights, and a very different list of top universities comes out.
The Times Higher Education system scores each university using five components: teaching, research, citation impact, international outlook, and industry income. Teaching, research, and citations each get 30 percent. The other two share the remaining 10 percent. Those numbers have been used for years without much debate.
Four formulas, five factors
Three alternative formulas are tested alongside the official one. Formula A gives 70 percent of the score to citation impact alone. Formula B gives 60 percent to teaching environment. Formula C pushes most weight toward international openness and industry ties, two factors the current system almost ignores.

Which universities move, and how far
Some universities barely move at all. Oxford stays at number one under every formula. MIT, Caltech, and Stanford stay in the top six no matter which weights are applied. These schools score well across all five factors, so shifting the formula does not hurt them much.
Peking falls from 17th to 65th under citation-heavy weighting, then climbs to 9th when teaching counts most. Chinese universities have long focused on enrolling students rather than producing widely cited research, which likely explains the drop. NUS Singapore climbs to 4th under the international formula because Singapore has spent decades recruiting foreign faculty and students as part of its economic strategy.

Geography shapes who gains and who loses
The shifts are not random. Under a citation-heavy formula, UK universities gain the most, moving up around 12 positions on average within the top 100. Universities in China drop the most. Under the international formula the picture flips: schools in Singapore, Hong Kong, Switzerland, and Australia move up while many large American universities fall.
British research funding has been tied to citation output for decades, so UK institutions were already built around that measure. Small city-states and wealthy neutral countries gain under international metrics because they have far fewer barriers to attracting foreign students and faculty than larger countries with stricter immigration rules.

A stable gap across 14 years
Looking across all 14 years of data, the pattern holds up consistently. Under the official formula, between 12 and 15 of the top 20 universities are in the United States every single year. Under the international formula, that number drops to somewhere between 7 and 10, and Europe and Asia-Pacific consistently fill more seats.
The gap has not closed over 14 years. One possible reason is that the formula was designed when American and British research universities set the standard for what a world-class institution looked like. Because the formula has not changed much since then, the rankings keep producing the same result.

None of this is meant to say the rankings are wrong. The point is simply that they are not neutral. A university that sits at 40th place today might sit at 15th under a different formula, without anything on campus actually changing. Students, governments, and institutions that use these numbers to make real decisions are worth reminding of that.
