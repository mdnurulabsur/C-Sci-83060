For this dashboard, I wanted to show MoMA's exhibition history in a way that someone with no data background could actually read. The audience I had in mind was someone who maybe studied art history or just likes museums, not someone who looks at dashboards every day. So the goal was: you open it, you immediately get the point.

I started with hierarchy because that felt like the most important thing. To achieve this, I put four big numbers at the top: total exhibitions, average duration, solo shows, and the number that included women artists. I wanted those to be the first things users see, so I used a big font, a heavier weight, and clear separation from everything else. The main line chart occupies most of the middle, since the story I wanted to tell was about time and how MoMA's programming grew and shifted over 60 years. Finally, the three smaller panels at the bottom provide more detail, though they're not the focus.

For typography, I kept it as simple as I could. Bold for numbers, regular weight for descriptions, and uppercase for the small category labels. That way, one can tell immediately what the data is and what is just context, without needing colors or extra fonts to do the job.

Spacing was something I kept going back to. Everything uses the same padding inside panels and the same gap between sections. It sounds simple, but it's actually what makes the whole thing feel organized rather than random.

For color, I used white and light grey as the base, one blue for all the exhibition data, and green only for the women's representation panel, because that's measuring something different. I didn't want color to be decorative; I wanted it to carry meaning.

The two dropdowns sit right under the header. Decade updates every panel at once. Nationality zooms into the nationality bars and displays the count and percentage for the selected country. The user can combine them.

Tools used: I opened the CSV in Python first because the dataset had one row per person, not per exhibition, so I needed to aggregate it before calculating anything. Once I had the numbers, I built the dashboard in HTML, CSS, and JavaScript using Chart.js for the charts. I used Claude by Anthropic toward the end to help fix bugs, double-check the data accuracy, and clean up the code.
